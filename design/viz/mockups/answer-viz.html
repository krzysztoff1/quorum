Food-tech personalization

What do personalization systems in food tech look like, what do they earn, and what constrains them?

11 sources cited of 36 read
3 critics · 2 resolved · 1 open

Food-tech personalization has converged on embeddings plus LLM-written profiles, with allergy rules kept outside the model. Its measured payoff is small in grocery, 1–2% of sales, and the money is made in retail media, while diet data sits in a gray zone the FTC, not HIPAA, polices.

The sales lift is real, and small in grocery

McKinsey's benchmark puts personalization at 5–15% of revenue across retail, but only 1–2% of total sales for grocers. Vendor case studies report far larger effects that no filing confirms.

The money is in retail media, not data sales

Behavioral data is monetized inside the platform through ads, not sold. All three delivery platforms now book at least $1B a year in ad revenue.

Subscriptions monetize personalization indirectly, and leak: two-thirds of DashPass sign-ups are gone within six months.

Architecture converged on a hybrid

DoorDash, Uber Eats and Instacart moved from collaborative filtering to two-tower embeddings with bandits for exploration, and added LLM-written preference profiles for users with thin histories. Allergy and diet rules are filtered outside the model, not learned.

Diet data sits in a legal gray zone

Most diet and delivery apps are not covered by HIPAA. The FTC has filled the gap, and its remedies escalated from fines to destroying the models trained on the data.

Where users do enter allergies, the field often goes nowhere: most delivery listings checked had no allergy intake at all.
